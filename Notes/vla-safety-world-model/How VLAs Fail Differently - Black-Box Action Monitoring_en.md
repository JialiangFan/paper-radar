# How VLAs Fail Differently: Black-Box Action Monitoring Reveals Architecture-Specific Failure Signatures

> Authors: Krishnam Gupta (Independent Research), 2026, arXiv:2605.28726

## Topic
Black-box VLA action monitoring

## Background
VLA models map vision and language end-to-end to robot actions, but what happens between the model's output layer and the robot's motors is largely unstudied. OpenVLA's deployment script sends raw outputs to motors with zero bounds checking, pi0 streams actions over WebSocket without validation, and LeRobot's `EEBoundsAndSafety` processor is imperative, non-composable, and easy to omit — all resting on the implicit assumption that a good model produces safe actions. The paper runs black-box action monitoring across three architectures (VQ-BeT, Diffusion Policy, ACT) on two tasks (PushT and ALOHA 14-DOF bimanual manipulation) over 450 episodes, and finds that VLA failures at the motor-command level are architecture-specific and predictable.

## Limitations & Research Problem
- **Limitation:** Existing safety approaches are either training-time (SafeVLA, RobustVLA, requiring retraining), inference-time but dependent on dynamics models or architecture-internal access (AEGIS's CBF-QP, SafeDiffuser/CoDiG's embedded barriers, SafeDec's constrained decoding, ATACOM's manifold projection), or failure detection requiring trained auxiliary models over internal features (SAFE, FIPER, Sentinel). Training-free, model-access-free action-space monitoring is essentially unexplored.
- **Limitation:** The discrete/continuous split among VLA architectures is well known, yet no prior work has empirically compared monitor effectiveness across these families **on the same task under an identical evaluation protocol**, nor shown which monitoring signals predict failure for which architectures.
- **Problem:** Does velocity checking — the most common safety mechanism in deployed VLA code — actually predict task failure? And is there a universal monitor, or must monitor selection be architecture-matched?

## Contributions
- **SafeContract**: a training-free, black-box (no model access, no retraining) action-monitoring and enforcement toolkit with conformal calibration and CUSUM shift detection, adding <13 µs per inference step (<0.001% of VLA inference time); open-sourced.
- **A universal failure predictor**: direction reversal rate is the only monitor effective across all three architectures — AUROC = 0.93 (VQ-BeT), 0.79 (Diffusion), 0.91 (ACT), all p < 0.001. A high reversal rate indicates the policy is oscillating rather than progressing, an architecture-agnostic signature of indecision.
- **A discrete-to-continuous gradient**: jerk monitoring is predictive only for discrete-token architectures, with AUROC falling 0.88 (VQ-BeT) → 0.69 (ACT, chunk-boundary effects) → 0.41 (Diffusion). The gradient reflects the action generation mechanism, not the task.
- **A strong negative result on the most common safety mechanism**: velocity violations are non-predictive everywhere (AUROC 0.41–0.69) and carry effectively zero signal for the continuous family (0.52 for ACT = chance, 0.41 for Diffusion = below chance). ACT fails on 38% of episodes (19/50), but its failures are *behavioral* — physically valid actions directed at the wrong target — and are invisible to velocity checking.
- **A two-family pattern**: the discrete family (autoregressive, VQ-VAE) exhibits quantization artifacts as high jerk, high reversal rates, and velocity spikes, with jerk and reversal strongly predictive (AUROC > 0.85); the continuous family (diffusion, flow matching, action chunking) produces smooth trajectories whose primary failure mode is behavioral error, where reversal remains predictive but jerk drops to chance. On identical data, VQ-BeT produces 2.4× more velocity violations than Diffusion (1,847 vs. 772), 2.7× higher mean jerk RMS (21.6 vs. 8.0), and near-zero stall steps (1 vs. 117).
- **Actionable architecture-matched monitoring recommendations**: Diffusion — reversal rate plus momentum coherence, avoid jerk/velocity/spectral; VQ-BeT — reversal rate plus jerk, avoid stall; ACT — reversal rate plus momentum/spectral, avoid velocity/stall.

## Methodology
- **Safety contracts.** A contract C = (l, u, v_max) specifies per-joint bounds and a velocity limit. Enforcement clips to bounds, clamps velocity, then re-clips (necessary because velocity clamping can push actions back outside bounds). All violations are logged with timestep, dimension, and magnitude.
- **Conformal calibration.** Hand-tuned thresholds are unreliable (a naive v_max = 0.05 clips a significant fraction of expert actions). Split-conformal prediction is used instead: demonstration episodes are split 80/20, nonconformity scores are computed on the calibration set, and the (1−α)(1+1/n) quantile yields finite-sample coverage. At α = 0.05 this achieves 97.9% holdout coverage while being 25% tighter than a 4σ heuristic; velocity limits are set per-joint at the 99th percentile of consecutive action deltas.
- **Five per-episode health metrics.** (1) direction reversal rate — fraction of (timestep, joint) pairs where the action changes sign; (2) jerk RMS — root-mean-square of the third derivative of the action trajectory; (3) momentum coherence — cosine similarity between consecutive action deltas, measuring trajectory smoothness; (4) spectral energy ratio — fraction of signal energy at low frequencies; (5) stall detection — consecutive steps with displacement below a threshold τ. Each metric is computed per episode and correlated with task success via AUROC.
- **CUSUM shift detection.** Conformal p-values feed a CUSUM detector S_t = max(0, S_{t−1} + 1[p_t < α] − α), firing an alarm when S_t > h, with formal false-alarm bounds that ad-hoc EWMA cannot provide.
- **Experimental design.** A same-dataset cross-architecture comparison on PushT (seeds 0–199, contract bounds [0, 512], v_max = 30 px/step, success = coverage ≥ 0.95, n = 200 per condition) isolates architecture effects; the study is then generalized to ACT on ALOHA 14-DOF cube transfer (n = 50). Platform: NVIDIA A40 with LeRobot pretrained checkpoints.
- **Enforcement without degradation.** Across all 450 episodes, monitoring and enforcement cause no statistically significant task degradation (Fisher's p = 0.92 / 0.76 / 0.68), indicating that conformally calibrated bounds are tight enough to be informative yet loose enough to avoid clipping correct actions.

## Relevance to STL×VLA
The safety contract here is, in substance, a set of time-invariant safety invariants over the action space — G(l ≤ a_t ≤ u) and G(|Δa_t| ≤ v_max) — but the paper never formalizes them in a temporal logic, provides no robustness semantics, and uses no nested temporal operators (until, bounded eventually). It is therefore conformally calibrated threshold monitoring with statistical (CUSUM) alarms rather than STL runtime monitoring. It does both detect and intervene (bound clipping and velocity clamping), though the author explicitly frames SafeContract as monitoring infrastructure rather than a safety guarantee, composable with CBF-based constraints as the innermost layer of a defense-in-depth stack.

The most useful interface to an STL×VLA agenda is the paper's negative result: pointwise magnitude constraints (velocity) do not predict failure, while *trajectory-shape* metrics do — direction reversal rate (oscillation), momentum coherence (directional persistence), and stall detection (absence of progress). These are naturally temporal properties: oscillation as repeated sign crossings within a bounded window, stalling as F_[0,T](progress), coherence as sustained direction over a window. The paper collapses each into a scalar per-episode statistic scored by AUROC, discarding temporal structure and any localization of *when* the property is violated. Writing these signatures as time-windowed STL formulas and using quantitative robustness as a continuous failure score is a concrete direction the paper itself does not take.

A second gap is one the author concedes: action-space monitoring is blind to semantic correctness. ACT's failures are physically valid actions aimed at the wrong target, and semantic correctness is a property of the (action, observation, instruction) triple rather than of the action alone. That is precisely the region where STL specified over observation- and task-level signals — rather than over the raw action signal — would extend beyond what black-box action monitoring can reach, and it constitutes a clear differentiating argument for STL×VLA over pure action monitoring.

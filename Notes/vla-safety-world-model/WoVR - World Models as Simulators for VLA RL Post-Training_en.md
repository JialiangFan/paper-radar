# WoVR: World Models as Reliable Simulators for Post-Training VLA Policies with RL

> Authors: Zhennan Jiang, Shangqing Zhou, Yutong Jiang, Zefang Huang, Mingjie Wei, Yuhui Chen, Tianxing Zhou, Zhen Guo, Hao Lin, Quanlu Zhang, Yu Wang, Haoran Li, Chao Yu, Dongbin Zhao (2026, arXiv 2602.13977)

## Topic

Hallucination-aware world-model RL for VLA

## Background

Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models are predominantly trained by imitation learning, which caps their performance ceiling at the quality and coverage of the demonstration corpus. RL post-training can break that ceiling, but on-policy RL demands massive parallel environment interaction that is infeasible on physical robots, while conventional simulators remain hard to align with real-world dynamics. Replacing real interaction with a learned world model as a generative simulator is therefore an attractive route — except that learned world models are not faithful simulators: closed-loop imagined rollouts hallucinate, producing visually plausible but physically incorrect transitions and even spurious success signals.

## Limitations & Research Problem

- **Limitation:** Prior world-model-based RL methods (WMPO, World-Env, Prophet) treat the world model as a drop-in simulator and never confront hallucinated dynamics directly. In closed-loop autoregressive rollouts, error compounds through two coupled channels: (i) *autoregressive feedback*, where the model conditions on its own generated frames and amplifies small early errors, and (ii) *distribution shift*, where the improving policy drifts away from the data used to train the world model, inducing out-of-distribution prediction failures. When hallucinated trajectories drive optimization, RL is incentivized to exploit systematic model error rather than genuine task progress — empirically, WMPO yields zero gain on LIBERO-Long.
- **Problem:** If world models inevitably hallucinate, how can RL remain reliable under imperfect imagined dynamics? The authors reframe this as a *reliability* problem rather than a *modeling* problem, arguing hallucination must be controlled at three interlocking levels: controllable simulator design, reliable interaction protocol, and policy-model alignment.

## Contributions

- Identifies hallucination in closed-loop imagined interaction as the central reliability bottleneck of world-model-based RL for VLA, attributing it to autoregressive error accumulation and policy-induced distribution shift.
- Proposes WoVR, a hallucination-aware framework that jointly regulates simulator controllability, imagined-interaction protocol, and policy-simulator alignment, enabling stable on-policy optimization entirely in imagination.
- Delivers a state-of-the-art action-conditioned video world model, dominating EVAC, Cosmos-Predict2, and OpenSora (WMPO's backbone) on LPIPS/FID/FVD/FloLPIPS with a widening margin as rollout horizon grows, at 23 FPS throughput versus OpenSora's 7 FPS.
- Large LIBERO gains: under one-trajectory SFT, average success rate rises 40.5% to 69.5% (WMPO 50.9%, online GRPO 44.6% under an identical 2,500-trajectory budget); under full-trajectory SFT, 88.1% to 96.0%.
- Real-robot transfer on two platforms: +28.9 points on Franka Emika Panda (51.1% to 80.0%) and +13.4 points on the noisier AgileX Piper, with no additional online interaction; swapping the backbone to π0.5 (optimized with Flow-SDE) still yields +22.3 points, showing architecture-agnosticism.
- Released within the RLinf ecosystem (github.com/RLinf/RLinf, HuggingFace RLinf/rlinf-wovr).

## Methodology

- **Stabilized action-conditioned world model (simulator-level control).** A Wan2.2-TI2V-5B video diffusion backbone is reformulated as an action-conditioned generator via *dual-channel action injection*: actions modulate denoising features through timestep-conditioned normalization and simultaneously replace text embeddings in cross-attention to supply global action context, preserving the DiT structure while granting frame-level controllability. Trained with a Rectified Flow objective; inference needs only five diffusion steps plus a 3D VAE, which is why the ~5B model outruns the ~1.3B OpenSora.
- **First-frame anchoring plus noisy context augmentation.** Each autoregressive step conditions on `[o_0, o_{t-c:t}]` (one persistent reference frame plus four memory frames): the fixed anchor constrains global layout and appearance while memory frames carry local dynamics. During training, non-reference context latents are mildly noised (the anchor stays clean), closing the train-inference gap and discouraging brittle copying from self-generated frames. Ablations show removing either component induces spatial drift and object disappearance over long horizons.
- **Keyframe-Initialized Rollouts, KIR (interaction-level reshaping).** Rather than always starting from `o_0`, a portion of imagined rollouts is initialized at task-critical intermediate states — particularly failure states of the current policy — using context `[o_0, o_{T_KIR-3:T_KIR}]`. This shortens the effective prediction depth so the model need not hallucinate a long prefix before reaching decisive contacts. A single task-level keyframe index is fixed per task rather than cherry-picked per trajectory, keeping KIR cheap and reproducible.
- **GRPO with valid-step normalization.** Policy updates use GRPO over imagined rollouts, with trajectory-length normalization counting only valid timesteps up to first success. This complements KIR: keyframe-initialized rollouts resolve in fewer valid steps, so gradients are dominated by short, task-critical segments rather than long, drift-prone continuations.
- **PACE: policy-aligned co-evolution (alignment-level regulation).** An initial WM_Base is trained on 1,500 base-policy trajectories; after a first stage of imagined policy optimization, 1,000 additional rollouts from the evolved policy refine it into WM_Evo. The refinement is deliberately *low-frequency*, unlike classical MBRL's continuous dynamics updates — it requires no ongoing human supervision or environment resets, yet restores simulator-policy alignment. Training curves show imagined success rate over-optimistically outrunning real evaluation before the PACE transition and re-converging afterwards. Ablations isolate the effect: adding more *base-policy* data (1,500 to 2,500) lifts the two-suite average only 75.8% to 77.5%, whereas policy-aligned data reaches 82.5%; removing KIR alone costs 2.8 points.
- **Reward modeling and systems.** Two options are supported: a lightweight ResNet binary success classifier (HiL-SERL style) and a Qwen3-VL + LoRA dense reward that casts progress as ordinal visual estimation over 11 levels (labels derived from temporal proximity to first success). Dense reward improves early sample efficiency but converges to the same final performance at roughly 3x the rollout cost, so sparse reward is used throughout the main experiments. Following RLinf-VLA, Generation/Simulator/Training are collocated on shared GPUs with offload/onload restricted to the boundaries of the rollout phase, avoiding per-interaction parameter transfers.

## Relevance to STL×VLA

WoVR is currently the most complete reliability-engineering treatment of the "learned world model as a training harness for VLA" thesis, and its central claim — that the bottleneck when a world model serves as an RL simulator is *reliability*, i.e. systematic mismatch between imagined and real outcomes, not raw generative fidelity — is an informal statement of exactly the problem class formal specification languages target. Yet WoVR contains no safety constraint, constraint satisfaction, or formal specification of any kind. Its reliability is secured purely by architectural means (first-frame anchoring, noisy context), sampling means (KIR), and data means (PACE), while task success collapses into a ResNet binary classifier or a VLM's 0-10 progress score — neither of which can express temporal structure or distinguish hallucinated success from genuine success.

The openings are unusually explicit. The paper's own Limitations section concedes two gaps: no formal characterization of how hallucination propagates into policy optimization, and no regret bound for the policy learned in imagination relative to the optimal policy in the real environment. Signal Temporal Logic is a natural instrument here — STL robustness could act as a monitor on imagined rollouts, vetoing spurious success signals by robustness margin rather than classifier probability, and providing a route toward the quantitative characterization the authors say they lack. Second, KIR's "task-critical state" is currently a hand-set per-task index; defining it as the moment where the robustness margin of a temporal specification approaches zero would make keyframe selection principled and automatic. Third, the hand-designed ordinal progress dense reward is a coarse surrogate for what STL robustness supplies directly and continuously. Finally, the real-world failure modes catalogued in Appendix E — premature gripper opening, failure to release after reaching the target, incomplete contact sequences, incorrect placement pose — are almost line-for-line expressible as temporal-logic specifications, suggesting that what this pipeline is missing is a spec-level monitoring and evaluation layer, not a larger video model.
